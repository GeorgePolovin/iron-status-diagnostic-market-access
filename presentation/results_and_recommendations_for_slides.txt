RESULTS AND RECOMMENDATIONS: AT-HOME IRON-STATUS TEST
Market-access and customer-fit analysis (slide-by-slide content brief)
=====================================================================

How to use: each section is one slide. HEADLINE = slide title. WHY = the evidence.
DECISION = the business decision it supports. Figure/table references point to outputs/.
Every number comes from the computed analysis tables. Do not round further or change values.

Data: NHANES 2017-Mar 2020 (real, measured labs, n=10,546) | MEPS 2023 (real national survey,
14,768 adults) | Synthea (fully synthetic EHR, 1,171 patients; workflow logic only).
No number describes the performance of any actual test.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 1 - THE RECOMMENDED DECISION (executive summary)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Launch as an at-home screen-and-refer test for menstruating women, entering through athletes

DECISION:
  1. Launch direct-to-consumer for non-pregnant menstruating women 18-49.
  2. Enter through athletes and highly active women (beachhead), then expand to all menstruating women.
  3. A positive or borderline result routes to a confirmatory blood ferritin test (telehealth / retail lab).
     The test screens and refers. It does not diagnose.
  4. Set the ferritin cutoff (15 vs 30 ng/mL) from a validation study, not from market size.
  5. Defer pregnancy, kidney disease and heart failure to later, clinician-supervised indications.
  6. Add a subsidised / community-health channel for lower-income women after launch.

WHY (one line each):
  - 3.5M US women 20-44 have depleted iron but normal hemoglobin; today's workflow misses them.
  - Need is 15-17% in menstruating and lower-income women vs 1% in men.
  - Athletes: same need, more often hidden (non-anemic), most able to pay, most affected by symptoms.
  - Young women delay care more and are less attached to a regular clinician: convenience sells.
  - Cutoff choice changes the market 2.5x.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 2 - THE UNMET NEED  [Figure F01, Table T03]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: 55% of women with depleted iron are not anemic

WHY:
  - 13.5% of non-pregnant US women 20-44 have depleted iron stores (ferritin <15 ng/mL), about 6.4M women.
  - 55% of them have normal hemoglobin (95% CI 47.5-62.5%).
  - That is 3.5M women (7.4% of the age group) whose deficiency is invisible to a hemoglobin/CBC check.
  - At the broader ferritin <30 threshold: 11.9M women with low iron and no anemia.
  - With an inflammation adjustment (CRP): 9.2M. The finding holds under every definition tested.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - The product-market gap exists: there is a large group that standard care does not catch.
  - Position the test upstream of the CBC, as the trigger that sends someone for a ferritin test.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 3 - WHO HAS THE NEED  [Figure F02, Table T02]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Depleted iron concentrates in women of reproductive age

WHY (share with ferritin <15 ng/mL, 95% CI):
  - Lower-income women 18-49 ..... 16.5% (13.1-19.8)
  - Menstruating women 18-49 ..... 15.3% (12.7-17.8), about 7.5M women: the largest market
  - Non-pregnant women 18-49 ..... 13.9% (11.6-16.2)
  - Highly active women 18-49 .... 13.2% (9.3-17.0)
  - Pregnant women 20-44 ......... 24.2% (13.9-34.6), small sample (n=76)
  - All non-pregnant adults ...... 4.9%
  - Men 18+ ...................... 1.0%

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - Target women 18-49. Men are not a market for this product.
  - Menstruation is the core driver, so menstruating women are the core market.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 - WHY ATHLETES ARE THE BEACHHEAD  [Table T02]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Athletes have the same need, more hidden deficiency, and the most ability to pay

WHY:
  1. Same need: 13.2% of highly active women have depleted iron, the same as other women their age.
  2. More often hidden: 62% of depleted, highly active women are NOT anemic, vs 50% of all women 18-49.
     (Points the same way but not statistically significant: CIs 47-78% vs 44-57%.) A blood count is the
     least likely way to find their deficiency.
  3. Able and willing to pay:
     - 44% have household income at least 4x the poverty line (vs 35% of women 18-49)
     - only 8.6% are uninsured (vs 14.6%), the best coverage of any segment
  4. The symptoms cost them more: non-anemic iron deficiency shows up as fatigue and lower endurance.
     A sedentary person who is tired can live with it. An athlete who has to train and compete notices
     it and acts on it. That makes the problem visible and urgent, and makes them more willing to pay.
     (Domain rationale, consistent with sports-medicine evidence; this data does not measure
     performance or willingness to pay, so it needs a WTP study.)
  5. Reachable: teams, clubs, coaches, collegiate programs and sports-medicine clinics make them easy
     to find, and early wins become case studies.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - Enter through athletes and highly active women at premium DTC pricing.
  - Use team/club partnerships and performance channels.
  - Expand to all menstruating women 18-49 once proven.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 - THE CUTOFF IS A STRATEGIC DECISION  [Figure F04, Table T04]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Flagging ferritin <30 makes the target group 2.5x larger

WHY (non-pregnant women 20-44):
  - ferritin <15 ng/mL (WHO depleted stores): 13.5%
  - ferritin <30 ng/mL: 33.4%
  - ferritin <50 ng/mL: 55.1%
  - A higher cutoff grows the market but sends more people to confirmatory testing and adds false positives.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - Do not pick the cutoff for market size.
  - Run a validation study against blood ferritin at both 15 and 30 ng/mL, choose the cutoff on
    accuracy, then size the market. This is the first gating decision before launch.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 - NEED VERSUS ACCESS  [Figure F03, Table T02]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Lower-income women have the highest need and the weakest coverage

WHY:
  - Lower-income women: 16.5% depleted iron and 27.4% uninsured (vs 13.3% of all adults).
  - They are the only segment in the high-need, low-access quadrant.
  - Highly active women sit at the opposite corner: high need, 8.6% uninsured.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - DTC pricing will not reach the highest-need group.
  - After launch, add a second channel: community health centers, Medicaid managed care, or employer
    programs. Build the case on cost per case found versus lab-first screening.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 - WHERE THE CURRENT PATHWAY FAILS  [Table T06]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Young women are less connected to care, which makes convenience the value proposition

WHY (MEPS 2023):
  - Women 18-49 with a usual source of care: 64.7% (vs 70.6% of all adults)
  - Women 18-49 who delayed medical care: 10.0% (vs 7.2% of all adults)
  - Women 18-49 who couldn't afford care: 5.4% (vs 4.0% of all adults)

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - A home test removes the first visit for the people least likely to make it.
  - Build the product with a one-step result → telehealth consult → lab order flow.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 - DIAGNOSED AND CHRONIC PATIENTS ARE A DIFFERENT CHANNEL  [Figure F06, Table T06]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Diagnosed patients already see clinicians about twice as often

WHY (mean office visits per year):
  - All adults 7.3 | Anemia diagnosis 14.8 | Fatigue/dizziness/hair loss 14.8 | Heart failure 16.4
    | Kidney disease 23.1 (small sample)
  - For them the problem is not getting to a clinician; it is that no one orders a ferritin test.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - These patients are reached through clinicians, not DTC. That is a later, clinician-recommended
    channel, not part of the first launch.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 9 - CHRONIC CONDITIONS NEED A DIFFERENT PRODUCT  [Figure F07, Tables T02/T07]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: Heart failure and kidney disease are majority publicly insured, with a different iron definition

WHY:
  - Heart failure: 65% public-only insurance; kidney disease: 60% (small sample).
  - About half of heart-failure adults (51%) are iron-deficient by the heart-failure definition
    (ferritin <100, or 100-299 with transferrin saturation <20%). Ferritin <15 misses them (2.5%).
  - Annual out-of-pocket spend: heart failure $3,782 vs $1,106 for all adults.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - Do not pursue these at launch. They need a test that reads higher ferritin ranges and works under
    inflammation, plus Medicare/Medicaid-grade evidence. Treat it as a future monitoring indication.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 - WHERE THE TEST SITS IN CARE  [Figure F08, Table T09; synthetic data]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
HEADLINE: 97% of target-condition visits are routine ambulatory or wellness visits

WHY (Synthea synthetic EHR, illustration of workflow logic):
  - 73.7% ambulatory + 23.4% wellness visits; 0.1% inpatient.
  - Only 6% of synthetic anemia patients had a blood count in the year before diagnosis.
  - The synthetic records contain no ferritin tests at all.

DECISION IT SUPPORTS:
  - The confirmatory hand-off belongs in primary care, telehealth or retail labs, not hospitals.
  - Next step: repeat this pathway analysis on real claims data to measure the actual delay to diagnosis.

SLIDE 12 - RECOMMENDATIONS AND NEXT ACTIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Priority 1 (0-12 months)
  1. Validation study vs blood ferritin at 15 and 30 ng/mL; choose the cutoff on accuracy. [Clinical]
  2. Launch the DTC screen-and-refer kit for menstruating women 18-49. [Product + clinical]
  3. Athlete beachhead: team/club/sports-medicine partnerships, premium positioning,
     willingness-to-pay + usability study. [Commercial]
  4. Telehealth / retail-lab partner for result → consult → lab order. Track % of positives who
     complete confirmatory testing. [Commercial]
Priority 2 (6-24 months)
  5. Community-health / Medicaid / employer pilot for lower-income women; measure cost per case
     found. [Market access]
  6. Replicate the pathway analysis on real claims or EHR data. [Analytics]
Priority 3 (18+ months)
  7. Pregnancy: clinician-ordered adjunct only, after validation in pregnancy. [Clinical/regulatory]
  8. Heart failure / kidney disease: future monitoring indication; requires performance at higher
     ferritin ranges and under inflammation. [Clinical/regulatory]


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 13 - WHAT WE COMMIT TO, AND WHAT WE DON'T
---------------------------------------------------------------------
COMMIT TO:
  - A consumer brand for women 18-49, entered through athletes
  - A confirmatory-lab partnership built into the product
  - A validation study before any accuracy claim
  - A second, subsidised channel for lower-income women after launch
DO NOT:
  - Build a clinician sales force for the first launch
  - Claim to replace a CBC, blood ferritin or a clinician's evaluation
  - Pursue Medicare / kidney / heart-failure reimbursement at launch
  - Price only for affluent buyers and ignore the highest-need group


---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 14 - LIMITATIONS (keep on the slide)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - No test performance data: nothing here estimates the accuracy or clinical value of any product.
  - NHANES is a one-time snapshot: low ferritin today does not prove future anemia. Segments are
    self-reported; the pregnancy sample is small (n=76).
  - Athlete willingness-to-pay and performance impact are reasoned, not measured. They need a WTP study.
  - MEPS conditions are self-reported; several segments have fewer than 100 people (flagged).
  - Synthea is synthetic: it shows workflow logic, not real-world rates. Names are pseudonyms.
  - Segments overlap and are not additive; population counts are survey estimates with sampling error.
